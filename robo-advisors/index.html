Where robo fits vs. DIY

Robo-Advisors & Brokers: The Honest Trade-Off

A robo-advisor costs more than buying an index fund yourself. We’ll say that first, plainly — and then explain why it can still be the right choice for some investors.

The math is simple. Holding VTSAX or FZROX yourself costs you near zero. A typical robo-advisor adds about 0.25% a year on top of the funds it buys. On a large balance over decades, that gap is real money — run it through the calculator and you’ll see.

So why would anyone pay it? Because the most expensive mistake in investing isn’t a slightly higher fee — it’s panic-selling in a downturn, never rebalancing, or never starting at all. If automation is what keeps you invested and on course, 0.25% is a bargain for the behavior it buys. The honest framing: a robo-advisor is cheap insurance against yourself, and pure overhead if you didn’t need the insurance. Only you know which describes you.

Betterment

Fee: 0.25% annual advisory fee (digital plan)

Minimum: $0 to open, $10 to start investing

Best for: Hands-off investors who want automatic rebalancing and tax-loss harvesting without thinking about it

Betterment is the largest independent robo-advisor and the most polished version of the hands-off pitch: deposit money, answer a few questions, and it builds a diversified ETF portfolio it rebalances automatically. The 0.25% fee is roughly six times what you'd pay holding a total-market index fund yourself — that's the honest trade. What you get for it is behavioral guardrails, automated tax-loss harvesting in taxable accounts, and goal-based buckets that make it harder to tinker.

What works

  • Automatic rebalancing and dividend reinvestment
  • Tax-loss harvesting on taxable accounts at no extra cost
  • Clean goal-based interface that discourages tinkering

The trade-offs

  • 0.25% fee is ~6x a DIY three-fund portfolio
  • You don't pick the underlying funds
  • Premium tier (human advice) requires a large balance
Our take

Worth it if automation keeps you invested. Not worth it if you're already comfortable buying VTSAX or FZROX on a schedule.

Wealthfront

Fee: 0.25% annual advisory fee

Minimum: $500

Best for: Investors who want robo automation plus a high-yield cash account in one place

Wealthfront runs the same basic playbook as Betterment — diversified ETF portfolios, automatic rebalancing, tax-loss harvesting — with a stronger software bent. Its direct indexing (buying individual S&P 500 stocks to harvest more losses) kicks in at higher balances and is a real, if modest, tax edge. The cash account consistently pays competitive yields, which makes it a reasonable single home for emergency fund plus long-term money.

What works

  • Direct indexing at higher balances improves tax-loss harvesting
  • Competitive high-yield cash account
  • Good automated financial planning tools

The trade-offs

  • $500 minimum
  • No human advisors at any tier
  • 0.25% fee on money you could index yourself for ~0.04%
Our take

The strongest pure-software robo. Same caveat as all of them: the 0.25% only earns its keep if the automation changes your behavior.

M1 Finance

Fee: $3/month platform fee (waived above $10,000)

Minimum: $100 ($500 for retirement accounts)

Best for: DIY investors who want automation around their own fund picks rather than someone else's portfolio

M1 sits between a brokerage and a robo. You build a 'pie' of funds or stocks — say 60% VTI, 30% VXUS, 10% BND — and M1 automates the rest: every deposit buys whatever slice is underweight. It's the closest thing to a self-directed robo, and for index investors who already know what they want to hold, it removes the last excuse for not rebalancing.

What works

  • You choose the exact ETFs; M1 automates contributions and rebalancing
  • Fee is flat and waived above $10,000
  • Fractional shares make small contributions efficient

The trade-offs

  • No mutual funds — ETFs and stocks only
  • Limited trading windows (one or two per day)
  • No tax-loss harvesting
Our take

The best fit for readers of this site: you keep control of the funds, M1 handles the discipline.

Interactive Brokers

Fee: $0 commissions on US stocks/ETFs (IBKR Lite); mutual fund fees vary

Minimum: $0

Best for: Cost-obsessed investors and anyone who wants access to nearly every fund and market on earth

Interactive Brokers is a full brokerage, not a robo, and it earns its place here for one reason: it is relentlessly cheap. IBKR Lite offers commission-free US stock and ETF trades, the margin rates are the lowest in the industry by a wide margin, and the mutual fund marketplace includes thousands of no-transaction-fee funds. The interface assumes you know what you're doing.

What works

  • Lowest all-in costs of any major broker
  • Enormous fund and ETF selection, including international markets
  • Industry-best margin rates if you ever need them

The trade-offs

  • Interface is built for professionals and feels like it
  • No automated portfolio management on the basic platform
  • Customer support is functional, not hand-holding
Our take

The power-user choice. If 'minimize every basis point' is your religion, this is your church — just expect a learning curve.

SoFi Invest

Fee: 0.25% for automated investing; $0 commissions for self-directed

Minimum: $1

Best for: Beginners starting with small amounts who want investing, banking, and loans under one roof

SoFi Invest is the friendliest on-ramp on this list. The $1 minimum and fractional shares mean a college student can start a real portfolio with lunch money, and the automated option builds a sensible ETF mix. The catch is depth: the fund lineup is thinner than the big brokerages, and SoFi's own ETFs anchor the automated portfolios.

What works

  • $1 minimum and fractional shares
  • Access to human financial planners at no extra cost
  • Tight integration with SoFi banking products

The trade-offs

  • Automated portfolios lean on SoFi's own ETFs
  • No tax-loss harvesting
  • Fund research tools are thin compared to Fidelity or Schwab
Our take

A fine place to start; many investors eventually outgrow it. Starting matters more than optimizing, so that's not a knock.

The DIY alternative, in one paragraph

If you decide the automation isn’t worth the fee, the do-it-yourself version is genuinely simple: open a free brokerage account, buy a single target-date index fund (or a total-market fund plus a bond fund), set up an automatic monthly contribution, and turn off the app. That’s the entire robo-advisor service — diversification, rebalancing, discipline — minus the 0.25%, for anyone willing to do roughly an hour of setup and then nothing. Both paths are respectable. The unforgivable one is doing neither.

Go-anywhere value investing — stocks, bonds, cash, special situations — with capital preservation as the stated first goal.

Crescent is the rare fund whose manager brags about what he didn't buy. Steve Romick's mandate is absolute-return value: own equities when they're cheap, credit when it's dislocated, and cash — sometimes lots of it — when nothing clears the bar. The result over three decades is equity-like returns with shallower drawdowns, the promise kept in 2008 better than almost any stock-heavy peer.

The costs are equally explicit: a 1.05% fee, long bull-market stretches of trailing fully invested benchmarks while cash drags, and a strategy that lives or dies on one team's judgment. Crescent is best understood not as a VBIAX alternative but as paying for an investing philosophy — patience as a position — that indexes structurally cannot hold. Whether that's worth 1% a year is a question about you, not just the fund.

Best for

Investors who want a manager empowered to hold cash and wait.

Frequently asked questions

What is the expense ratio of FPACX?

FPACX (FPA Crescent Fund) has an expense ratio of 1.05%, which works out to roughly $105 per year for every $10,000 invested. That is high relative to index alternatives, and it compounds against you every year, so the fund needs to outperform just to break even versus a cheaper option.

What is the minimum investment for FPACX?

FPACX requires a minimum initial investment of $1,500.

What does FPACX invest in?

Go-anywhere value investing — stocks, bonds, cash, special situations — with capital preservation as the stated first goal. It falls in the balanced / allocation category and is an actively managed fund.

What are good alternatives to FPACX?

The funds most often compared with FPACX are VWELX, PRWCX, VBIAX — broadly similar exposure from different providers, with small differences in fee, minimum, and portability that usually come down to which brokerage holds your account.

How to pick one

Target-Date Funds: The One-Decision Portfolio

A target-date fund is a complete, self-managing portfolio in a single ticker. For most retirement savers, it’s not a compromise — it’s close to the ideal.

What they are

You pick the fund with the year closest to your expected retirement — 2050, say — and the fund does everything else: it holds a diversified mix of US stocks, international stocks, and bonds, rebalances itself automatically, and gradually shifts from aggressive to conservative as the target year approaches. That gradual shift is called the glide path. Decades from retirement, you’re ~90% stocks; at and beyond retirement, the fund has eased you into a bond-heavy mix designed to survive withdrawals.

The underrated superpower

The best thing about a target-date fund isn’t the asset allocation — it’s that there’s nothing to tinker with during a crash. Studies of investor behavior consistently find target-date owners earn returns much closer to their funds’ actual returns, because the structure quietly prevents the buy-high-sell-low mistakes that cost self-directed investors 1–2% a year.

How to choose: a short checklist

1. Insist on the index version. This is the single most important and most-missed step. Fidelity, for example, runs two nearly identically named series — Freedom Index (cheap, ~0.12%) and the original active Freedom funds (several times pricier). The same glide path at a third of the cost is free money; check that the word “Index” appears in the fund name, character by character.

2. Match the date to your real timeline, not your risk mood. The date is a proxy for how many years of growth you have left. Want more risk? Pick a later-dated fund deliberately. Want less? Pick an earlier one. Just don’t change it because last year’s returns were good or bad — that’s the exact behavior the fund exists to prevent.

3. Hold it as the whole account. A target-date fund is engineered to be your entire portfolio. Bolting on “a little extra” tech fund or individual stocks quietly cranks up your risk and defeats the glide path. If you want to do that, you don’t want a target-date fund — you want a hobby, which is fine, but be honest about which one you’re choosing.

4. Mind the account type. Target-date funds are ideal in 401(k)s and IRAs. In a taxable account, the all-in-one structure forfeits foreign tax credits and the ability to place assets tax-efficiently, and the automatic rebalancing can trigger taxable events — so DIY index funds often fit taxable money better.

The major index target-date series

All three big providers now offer all-index target-date funds at 0.08–0.12%. They differ only in minor glide-path and international-weight choices; the deciding factor is where your account lives.

If it’s your 401(k)’s only option, use it and capture the match; when you change jobs, the balance can roll to an IRA holding a cheaper index series.
